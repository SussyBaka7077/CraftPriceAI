================================================================
CRAFTPRICE AI — THE TEA STALL STORY (presentation script)
================================================================

THE SETUP
---------
Suppose I am opening a tea stall and I have to decide the price of
one cup. It costs me $12 to make one cup, and the stall across the
street sells it for $20. The question: at what price should I sell
my cup for MAXIMUM PROFIT?

CraftPriceAI answers this in three stages:
  STAGE 1 — LEARN   : build a "crystal ball" that predicts sales
  STAGE 2 — DECIDE  : try every sensible price, keep the best one
  STAGE 3 — SHOW    : present it to the seller on a dashboard

Note: the tea numbers below are a small toy example (5 weeks of
data) so every calculation fits on paper. The real project does
exactly the same logic with 12,600 rows and 23 inputs. Where the
real numbers differ, I say so.

================================================================
STAGE 1 — LEARN (build the crystal ball)
================================================================

STEP 1 — Write down the past
-----------------------------

Learned parameters — 40,662 total
5 weeks of my own sales history (the data):

  Week | Price charged | Glasses sold
    1   |     $15       |    100
    2   |     $18       |     82
    3   |     $21       |     58
    4   |     $24       |     34
    5   |     $27       |     16

  - Price  = the clue we know in advance  (technical name: FEATURE)
  - Glasses = the answer we want to predict (technical name: TARGET)
  - Predicting a NUMBER (not yes/no) is called REGRESSION.

STEP 2 — The train/test split (the exam it never studied)
----------------------------------------------------------
  - Weeks 1-5  -> used for TRAINING (the model learns from these)
  - Week 6     -> kept hidden as the TEST set (touched once, at the
                  very end, to grade the model fairly)

  Why split by TIME and not randomly? Because you cannot use next
  week's sales to learn this week's. Two of our inputs
  (previous_week_sales, rolling_4_week_sales) come from earlier
  weeks, so a random split would let the model peek at answers.
  The real project splits chronologically 70/15/15
  (modeling.py: chronological_split).

STEP 3 — The simple model: a straight line (regression)
-------------------------------------------------------
  Shape of the guess:   glasses = m * price + b

  We do NOT calculate m and b in our code. The imported regression
  library does all of it inside .fit(). Training just means
  "finding m and b" — the library finds them from the data.

  (Hand version, just to show what .fit() is doing internally:
   average price = 21, average glasses = 58
   m = -648/90 = -7.2   b = 209.2
   so: glasses = 209.2 - 7.2 * price
   read as: "every $1 on the price scares off about 7 people")

  IMPORTANT CLARIFICATION: this line is our BASELINE / contrgol
  model ("can a simple rule do it?"). The model we actually ship
  is XGBoost (Step 5). Real scores on the hidden test period:
     line      -> MAE 4.26
     XGBoost   -> MAE 4.14   (the shipped one)

STEP 4 — Grading the model: the three scores (metrics)
-------------------------------------------------------
  MAE  (Mean Absolute Error):
     "on a typical day I am off by about 1.4 glasses" (toy)
     real project: off by about 4.1 UNITS.

  RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error):
     same idea, but it punishes big blunders much more.

  R^2 (coefficient of determination):
     "price alone explains 99.7% of the up-and-down in my sales"
     (toy — my invented data is unrealistically clean)
     real project: R^2 = 0.468.

  THE HONEST LINE TO SAY (most students miss this):
     "0.468 sounds low, but the data has random noise baked into
      every sale (weather, moods...). A PERFECT model could only
      reach 0.566 on this dataset. So we capture 83% of everything
      knowable, and our MAE is within 6.6% of a perfect model.
      Adding more model complexity here would buy nothing."

STEP 5 — The real brain: XGBoost (450 tiny flowcharts)
------------------------------------------------------
  Why flowcharts (decision trees) and not just the line?
  NOT merely because "season matters" — a line could learn that by
  ADDING an effect ("June heat: -5 glasses"). The real reason is
  COMBINATIONS (technical term: interactions). A line can only add
  effects ("price -3, season -5"), it cannot say:

     "a $1 price rise hurts TWICE as much in June heat
      as it does in December"

  Every question inside a flowchart depends on the answers above
  it. That is what a line can never do. Our data generator builds
  demand by MULTIPLYING effects, and multiplication creates exactly
  these combinations.

  How they are built (gradient boosting — a relay race):
     - Round 0: start dumb — always guess the average (58)
     - Flowchart 1: "is price above $21?" ->
         No  weeks: average miss +22 -> nudge up
         Yes weeks: average miss -33 -> nudge down
     - Each nudge applied at only 4.5% strength (learning_rate)
     - Repeat 450 times (n_estimators = 450)
     - Each flowchart asks at most 5 questions (max_depth = 5)
     - Small steps, many rounds: we never overshoot badly.

  We do not code the relay race. xgb.fit() (modeling.py) IS the
  relay race. The finished brain is saved as
  artifacts/demand_model.joblib.

  One honest detail: in round 1, the $21 week was already perfect,
  but its teammates' correction dragged it to 69 (temporarily
  worse). Boosting improves the TEAM total; individual weeks can
  wobble. The small 4.5% steps are exactly what keeps that safe.

================================================================
STAGE 2 — DECIDE (turn the ball into a price)
================================================================

THE GOAL
--------
  Money kept = (price - $12 cost) x glasses sold
  (technical name: CONTRIBUTION)

  Two forces fight as price rises:
     profit per glass goes UP, glasses sold go DOWN.
  Their product is a HILL — we want the peak.

  Why not maximize glasses sold? Charge $1 — flood the street,
  stay poor. Selling more is not the goal; KEEPING more is.

THE RULES (constraints)
-----------------------
  1. "I want at least 20% of the FINAL PRICE to be profit"
     = margin ON PRICE (the correct reading — the one our code uses)
       price >= 12 / (1 - 0.20) = $15     check: 3/15 = exactly 20%

     THE TRAP (know this — it was a real bug in our project):
       20% ADDED TO COST (markup) gives 12 x 1.2 = $14.40 —
       but 2.40/14.40 = only 16.7% margin. You would quietly earn
       less than you asked for. Nothing crashes; the two numbers
       just measure different things. That is the most dangerous
       kind of bug. We fixed it AND wrote a regression test so it
       can never silently come back (pricing.py: _feasible_bounds).

  2. "Stay within 40% of my competitor's $20" (competitor band):
       from $12 to $28

  MY PLAYGROUND: $15 to $28.
  Notice: my own profit rule ($15) binds the bottom — his $12 band
  floor is weaker. The SELLER's rule is the stricter constraint.

TRY EVERY PRICE (grid search — no calculus)
---------------------------------------------
  Each dollar from $15 to $28: ask the ball for glasses, then
  compute money kept. Brute force, no failure modes.

  Price | Glasses | Profit/glass | Money kept
   $15  |  101.2  |     $3        |  $303.6
   $16  |   94.0  |     $4        |  $376.0
   $17  |   86.8  |     $5        |  $434.0
   $18  |   79.6  |     $6        |  $477.6
   $19  |   72.4  |     $7        |  $506.8
   $20  |   65.2  |     $8        |  $521.6   <- near-tie
   $21  |   58.0  |     $9        |  $522.0   <- WINNER (peak)
   $22  |   50.8  |    $10        |  $508.0
   $23  |   43.6  |    $11        |  $479.6
   $24  |   36.4  |    $12        |  $436.8
   $25  |   29.2  |    $13        |  $379.6
   $26  |   22.0  |    $14        |  $308.0
   $27  |   14.8  |    $15        |  $222.0
   $28  |    7.6  |    $16        |  $121.6

  In the code this is ONE function: optimize_price (pricing.py).
  predicted_demand     = model.predict(...)      (the ball)
  estimated_revenue    = price x demand          (money IN)
  estimated_contribution = (price - cost) x demand  (money KEPT)
  recommendation       = idxmax of contribution  (pick the winner)

THE HONESTY STEPS (the best part of the talk)
----------------------------------------------
  a) WHO decided — the model or a rule?
     $21 sits in the MIDDLE of the $15-28 playground: no rule
     forced it, the model genuinely picked it.
     (In the real project, ~60% of recommendations sit on the
      TOP EDGE of the band — and the app says so out loud:
      "your band set this price, not the model — widen it to
      check." That is diagnose_recommendation in pricing.py.
      Volunteering this is worth a lot.)

  b) Needle or plateau? The peak is FLAT:
     $19 -> 506.8, $20 -> 521.6, $21 -> 522, $22 -> 508.
     The true hilltop is $20.50, and whole dollars cannot land on
     it — so $20 and $21 are a TIE (40 cents apart). The honest
     answer is "$20-21", not one fake-precise number. My human
     instinct — "don't look pricier than the rival across the
     street" — is a valid tie-breaker. That is why the app shows
     the whole candidate table, not just one shiny number.

  c) Revenue would have LIED:
     $15 brings the most money IN (15 x 101 = $1,518)
     but keeps the LEAST profit ($303). We maximize money KEPT
     (contribution), not money in (revenue).

  d) The hidden pattern (elasticity — great viva moment):
     At $21, a 1% price rise (~$0.21) loses 7.2 x 0.21 = ~1.5
     glasses = 2.6% of sales. A 1% rise costs 2.6% of sales —
     customers are twitchy. This twitchiness is ELASTICITY (~2.6).
     Shortcut formula for this kind of stall:
        best price ~= cost + competitor/elasticity
                    = 12 + 20/2.6 ~= $19.7
     — which lands exactly on the $20-21 plateau we found by
     brute force. Two different roads, same hilltop.

  e) The honest range (prediction interval):
     We know from the hidden test week the ball is typically off
     by ~2 glasses. So we never say "58 glasses" — we say
     "about 58, likely 56-60." The app builds this range by
     measuring its own past misses, and self-checks the honesty:
     the 80% range actually covers 79.2% of the time.

================================================================
STAGE 3 — SHOW (the dashboard)
================================================================
  - Big numbers: "Charge $21 - expect ~58 glasses (range 56-60)
    - keep $522 - cost $12"
  - Picture 1: the SLIDE (glasses falling as price rises,
    playground shaded, my price and the recommendation marked)
  - Picture 2: the HILL (money kept vs price, peak visible at
    $20-21). Two SEPARATE charts — glasses and dollars are not
    comparable on one ruler (no dual-axis trickery).
  - The full candidate table with "$21 <- recommended" flagged.
  - Honest notes in plain words: "$20 is a near-tie — treat
    $20-21 as the answer." Plus the model's exam report: "typical
    miss ~2 glasses, measured on weeks it never studied."
  - Optional "why?" button (SHAP): splits the credit for the
    estimate among the inputs. In the toy, price did everything;
    in the real project there are 23 inputs and the split is
    genuinely interesting. It is the expensive part of the app,
    so it is behind a toggle and cached.

================================================================
THE CLOSING LINE
================================================================
  "It is not a sales predictor — it is a DECISION TOOL.
   The model is the engine; the price recommendation is the
   product. GPS predicts traffic; the driver picks the route."
